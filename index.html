Dirk Hartog’s plate, 1616

What does a flattened pewter plate prove?

What is agreed

On 25 October 1616 the Dutch East India Company (VOC) ship Eendracht, with Dirk Hartog as skipper, reached the island now called Dirk Hartog Island, in Shark Bay, Western Australia. The crew stayed about three days and sailed on 27 October towards Bantam, in Java.

The crew flattened a pewter plate, scratched an inscription into it, and nailed it to a post on high ground at the north end of the island, a place now called Cape Inscription.

The inscription, in translation, reads: “1616 the 25 October is here arrived the ship Eendraght of Amsterdam the uppermerchant Gillis Miebais of Liege skipper Dirck Hatichs of Amsterdam. The 27 ditto [we] set sail for Bantum the undermerchant Jan Stins, the first mate Pieter Dookes van Bil. Anno 1616.” (Western Australian Museum translation.)

In February 1697 another VOC expedition, under Willem de Vlamingh in the Geelvinck, found Hartog’s plate. De Vlamingh took it to Batavia (now Jakarta), and it was sent on to the Netherlands. It is now in the Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam. He left a new pewter plate in its place, copying Hartog’s words and adding his own.

In 1801 a French expedition found de Vlamingh’s plate; Captain Jacques Hamelin had it put back. In 1818 Louis de Freycinet returned and took it to France. It was found again in Paris in 1940, presented to the Australian Government in May 1947, and returned to Western Australia in 1950. It is in the Shipwreck Galleries of the Western Australian Museum, Fremantle.

Shark Bay is on Malgana Country. Neither plate mentions the island’s Traditional Owners.

Battye (ed.), The Cyclopedia of Western Australia (1912), public domain, via the National Library of Australia and Wikimedia Commons.

What they said. “1616 the 25 October is here arrived the ship Eendraght of Amsterdam the uppermerchant Gillis Miebais of Liege skipper Dirck Hatichs of Amsterdam. The 27 ditto [we] set sail for Bantum the undermerchant Jan Stins, the first mate Pieter Dookes van Bil. Anno 1616.”

Could they really have seen it? They were there, and the plate is the original object, not a later copy. It is very strong evidence that this ship stopped at this island on these dates. But it says nothing about what they did, what they saw, or whether they met anyone. It names five officers and no one else.

Why might they say it? To mark the visit for later ships and for the Company. It records names and a route, not a claim to the land. Students can ask why a crew would leave a message on an island they did not expect anyone to read for years.

Willem de Vlamingh, and the VOC officials at Batavia

The captain who found the plate, took it, and replaced it, and the officials who sent it home.

Source. De Vlamingh’s plate, 1697, Western Australian Museum; inscription and translation, and the letter from Batavia, as published by the Western Australian Museum, “Hartog & de Vlamingh.” No portrait of de Vlamingh is used. The image is a drawing of the 1697 inscription from The Cyclopedia of Western Australia (1912), public domain.

What they said. The 1697 plate copies Hartog’s words, then adds: “1697 the 4 February is here arrived the ship Geelvinck of Amsterdam, the commander and skipper Willem de Vlamingh of Vlieland …” and ends that they “sailed with our fleet to further explore the Southland and [are] destined for Batavia.” The officials at Batavia wrote that “this old dish which skipper Willem de Vlamingh brought us” would be sent home to the directors, “who will no doubt stand amazed that the same has for so long a series of years been preserved in spite of its being exposed to the influence of sky, rain and sun.”

Could they really have seen it? De Vlamingh’s crew saw the old plate with their own eyes, eighty-one years on, still near its post. The officials at Batavia saw the plate, but not the island. Neither could see what happened in 1616.

Why might they say it? De Vlamingh had orders to leave plates as markers. By copying Hartog’s words he turned a message into a memorial. The Batavia officials were sending a prized object to their employers. Students can ask: when you take an original away and leave a copy, what have you saved, and what have you changed?

Western Australian Museum

State museum, holds de Vlamingh’s plate · Web page, “Hartog & de Vlamingh”

The museum that looks after one of the two plates, explaining what both of them mean.

Source. Western Australian Museum, “Hartog & de Vlamingh,” museum.wa.gov.au (maritime archaeology, Cape Inscription pages). Quoted from the web page. The image is a document silhouette, not a facsimile.

What they said. “Hartog’s plate serves as proof of the first recorded landfall of a European on the coast of Australia.” Cape Inscription is “the provenance of the oldest European monument recovered from Australian soil.” On the two plates: “despite looking very similar, [they] had different meanings at the time they were created.”

Could they really have seen it? The museum was not there in 1616 or 1697. It has studied the plates, the posts and the old accounts, and searched the site. Its claim that the landfall was the “first recorded” is an interpretation, and other public sources disagree.

Why might they say it? To explain why its collection matters, clearly and proudly. Museums choose words like “first” and “oldest” because they help visitors see what is special. Students can set this beside the National Museum of Australia, which calls Hartog “only the second to record encountering the Australian continent.”

My finding

The three witness cards are the officers of the Eendracht (the plate itself), Willem de Vlamingh and the Batavia officials, and the Western Australian Museum. Further questions — first or second, the 1697 dates, and silence about the Malgana — are in Super Sleuths.

Why it still matters

Two pieces of flattened pewter are among the most famous objects in Australian history. The file is open because the words on them are short, because later people added meanings such as “first” and “discovery,” and because the plates are silent about the Malgana. What an object is said to prove often depends on who is holding it.

Super Sleuths

Dirk Hartog Island is on Malgana Country. This file uses published public records only. Amber marks what is still disputed, or what later writers added.

1. The notebook is written for this file

The typed page is a constructed student voice. The narrator is not a historical person. The facts in it (the hammered plate, five names, eighty-one years, Amsterdam, Fremantle, France) come from the agreed file.

Question. The narrator notices whose names are missing. Is that a historical observation, a feeling, or both?

2. First, or second?

The Western Australian Museum calls Hartog’s landing “the first recorded landfall of a European on the coast of Australia.” The National Museum of Australia says he was “the first European to set foot on the shores of Western Australia, and only the second to record encountering the Australian continent,” after Willem Janszoon in the Duyfken in 1606. The same Western Australian Museum page also says Hartog made “one of the first recorded landfalls by a European on the West Australian coastline.”

Question. Can both statements be true? What exactly would “first” have to mean for each one to be right?

3. 2 February or 4 February?

The Western Australian Museum says de Vlamingh’s first mate found Hartog’s plate on 2 February 1697. De Vlamingh’s own plate says the Geelvinck “arrived” on 4 February. Ships often lay off a coast before the official arrival was recorded.

Question. Does a two-day difference matter to the question this case asks? When would it matter?

4. Who found it in 1801?

The French expedition’s accounts differ. The Western Australian Museum says several crew, including Louis de Freycinet, found de Vlamingh’s plate and gave it to Captain Hamelin, who had it put back. The National Museum says de Freycinet found it. The Western Australian Museum also notes that the French accounts disagree about whether the plate went back on its old post or a new one.

Question. Why do small details, such as who picked something up, drift between retellings?

5. An island described as uninhabited

The National Museum describes the island in 1616 as “rugged and uninhabited.” The plate says nothing about people. Shark Bay is Malgana Country, and the Malgana are recognised today as Traditional Owners of the Shark Bay World Heritage Area with the Nanda and Yinggarda peoples.

Question. What is the difference between “no one was seen” and “no one belonged there”? Which one can a plate prove?

6. Sure, maybe, or a guess?

Sure: the Eendracht reached the island on 25 October 1616 and left on 27 October; the crew left an inscribed pewter plate; de Vlamingh removed it in 1697 and left a copy; the original is in the Rijksmuseum and the copy in Fremantle. Maybe: whether the landing was the first or second recorded European landing in Australia. A guess: what the crew saw, felt or met. The opener is not a source.

Question. Write a finding of no more than five sentences, and mark each sentence sure, maybe, or a guess.

Country, and care

The file’s point about silence is about the record, not a claim about what happened on the island in 1616. Source conflicts, flagged

First or second recorded European landing (WA Museum against National Museum). 2 or 4 February 1697 (WA Museum narrative against the 1697 plate). Who found the plate in 1801 (WA Museum against National Museum). The inscription’s spellings differ between transcriptions (Gillis or Gilles; Mibais or Miebais; Dookes or Doores; Lvik, Lvck or Luick, for Liège). The plate inscription spells the skipper “Dirck Hatichs.”
